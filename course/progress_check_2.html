    1. Interpreting Derivatives and Linear Approximation.

    In this problem, you will investigate how speed (in miles per hour) and the total time for a ten mile trip are related.
    🔗
    The total time (in seconds) for a trip of \(10 \)   miles at a constant speed of \(v\text{mph} \) (where \(v\gt{0} \)) is given by
    \begin{align*} T(v) &= \frac{10\text{mi}}{v\text{mph}}\cdot\frac{3600\text{s}}{1\text{hr}}\\ &= \frac{36,000}{v}\\ &= 36,000{v}^{-1}. \end{align*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Calculate and interpret (including units) \(T(60)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(T(60)=36000/60=600\text{,}\) which means that at \(60\)mph, the trip would take \(600\) seconds, or ten minutes.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Based on the physical context, would you expect \(T'(v)\) to be positive or negative? Explain.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since total time goes down as speed goes up, we should have \(T'(v)\lt{}0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Calculate \(T'(v)\text{.}\) (You may use either the limit definition of derivative or a shortcut that we have learned.)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the pattern/shortcut for powers of \(x\text{,}\) we know that \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}x^{-1}=-1\cdot{}x^{-2}\text{,}\) so \(T'(v)=-36000v^{-2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Using the limit definition, we have
    \begin{align*} T'(v) &= \lim_{u\to v}\frac{T(u)-T(v)}{u-v}\\ &= \lim_{u\to v}\frac{1}{u-v}\left(\frac{36000}{u}-\frac{36000}{v}\right)\\ &= \lim_{u\to v}\frac{1}{u-v}\left(\frac{36000v}{uv}-\frac{36000u}{uv}\right)\\ &= \lim_{u\to v}\frac{1}{u-v}\left(\frac{36000v-36000u}{uv}\right)\\ &= \lim_{u\to v}\frac{36000}{u-v}\left(\frac{v-u}{uv}\right)\\ &= \lim_{u\to v}\frac{-36000}{uv}\\ &= \frac{-36000}{v^{2}} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Calculate and interpret (including units) \(T'(60)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(T'(60)=-36000/(60^{2})=-36000/3600=-10\text{s}/\text{mph}\text{,}\) which says that at \(60\) miles per hour, increasing speed reduces the total trip time by \(10\) seconds per mile-per-hour.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    (i)

    Find an equation for the linear approximation of \(T\) at \(v=60\text{.}\) Call this function \(L_{T}(v)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The formula for a linear approximation at a point \(a\) is \(l(x)=f'(a)(x-a)+f(a)\text{,}\) so we have
    \begin{align*} L_{t}(v) &= T'(60)(v-60)+T(60)\\ &= -10(v-60)+600. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (ii)

    Use your linear approximation to approximate \(T(65)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We have
    \begin{align*} T(65) &\approx L_{T}(65)\\ & -10(65-60)+600.\\ & 550. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    (i)

    Calculate and interpret (including units) \(T(65)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(T(65)=36000/65=\frac{7200}{13}\approx 553.8461538\text{,}\) which means that at \(65\)mph, the trip would take about \(554\) seconds, or nine and one quarter minutes.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (ii)

    How does the actual value of \(T(65)\) compare with the value from the linear approximation? What is the magnitude of the error, and is the linear approximation an over- or under-estimate?
    🔗
    Solution.
    We know that \(T(65)=\frac{7200}{13}=553\,\frac{11}{13}\) and \(L_{T}(65)=550\text{,}\) so the (absolute) error is \(\abs{T(65)-L_{T}(65)}=3\frac{11}{13}\text{,}\) and \(L_{T}(v)\) underestimates \(T(v)\) at \(v=65\text{.}\) The relative error is \(\abs{\frac{L_{T}(65)-T(65)}{65-60}}\approx 0.769230769230769\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    Compare the value of the linear approximation at \(61\text{,}\) \(L_{T}(61)\text{,}\) with the value of \(T(61)\text{.}\) What is the magnitude of the error, and is the linear approximation an over- or under-estimate? How does this compare to the error at \(v=65\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We have \(T(61)=\frac{36000}{61}\approx 590.16393442623\) and \(L_{T}(61)=-10(61-60)+600=590\text{.}\) Here the error is \(L_{T}(61)-T(61)\approx -0.1639344\text{,}\) and \(L_{T}(v)\) underestimates \(T(v)\) at \(v=61\text{.}\) The relative error is \(\abs{\frac{L_{T}(61)-T(61)}{61-60}}\approx 0.163934426229508\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (h)

    Calculate and interpret (including units) \(T'(75)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(T'(75)=36000/(75^{2})=36000/5625=6.4\text{s}/\text{mph}\text{,}\) which says that at \(75\) miles per hour, increasing speed reduces the total trip time by \(6.4\) seconds per mile-per-hour.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (i)

    Would you expect \(T''(v)\) to be positive or negative? Explain.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Because \(T'(v)\lt{}0\) and because fewer seconds are saved at higher speed, \(T'(v)\) is increasing from more negative to less negative, and thus, \(T''(v)\gt{}0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Modeling and Optimization.

    A trough is made from a \(5\text{ft}\times{}20\text{ft}\) sheet of metal by folding two \(1\text{ft}\times{}20\text{ft}\) sections up at an angle \(\theta\) and attaching trapezoidal caps at the ends. A cross-section is shown in Figure 55.
    🔗

    (a)

    Express the total cross-sectional area, \(A(\theta)\text{,}\) and then the total volume of the trough, \(V(\theta)\text{,}\) in terms of trigonometric functions of \(\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Draw a vertical line through each of the corners on the base to divide the cross-section into a rectangle and two right triangles.
    🔗
    The total volume will be cross-sectional area times \(20\text{ft}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(h\) be the vertical height and \(w\) be the width of each triangular part of the cross-section. Then the area of the rectangular region will be \(3h\) and each triangular region will have area \(\frac{1}{2}wh\text{,}\) so the total cross-sectional area will be \(3h+wh\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In terms of sine and cosine, we have \(\sin(\theta)=\frac{h}{1}\) and \(\cos(\theta)=\frac{w}{1}\text{,}\) or equivalently \(h=\sin(\theta)\) and \(w=\cos(\theta)\text{.}\) Thus, the cross-sectional area is
    \begin{equation*} A(\theta)=3\sin(\theta)+\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta) \end{equation*}
    which makes the volume
    \begin{equation*} V(\theta)=60\sin(\theta)+20\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Calculate \(V'(\theta)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(V'(\theta)=60\cos(\theta)+20\left(\sin(\theta)\cdot{}-\sin(\theta)+\cos(\theta)\cos(\theta)\right)=60\cos(\theta)-20\sin^{2}(\theta)+20\cos^{2}(\theta)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find the angle \(\theta\) that maximizes the volume of the trough.
    🔗
    Hint.
    What will be true about the derivative at the point where the volume is highest?
    🔗
    You will need to use the identity \(\cos^{2}(\theta)+\sin^{2}(\theta)=1\) and solve a quadratic equation for \(\cos(\theta)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the Pythagorean identity for sine and cosine, we have \(\sin^{2}(\theta)=1-\cos^{2}(\theta)\text{,}\) so our derivative becomes
    \begin{align*} V'(\theta) &=60\cos(\theta)-20\sin^{2}(\theta)+20\cos^{2}(\theta)\\ &=60\cos(\theta)-20\left(1-\cos^{2}(\theta)\right)+20\cos^{2}(\theta)\\ &=60\cos(\theta)-20+20\cos^{2}(\theta)+20\cos^{2}(\theta)\\ &=60\cos(\theta)-20+40\cos^{2}(\theta). \end{align*}
    🔗
    If there is a maximum, then either \(V'(\theta)\) will be undefined at that point or \(V'(\theta)=0\text{.}\) Since sine and cosine are defined for all real \(\theta\text{,}\) we need to solve \(V'(\theta)=0\text{,}\) or
    \begin{equation*} 40\cos^{2}(\theta)+60\cos(\theta)-20=0. \end{equation*}
    If we let \(x=\cos(\theta)\text{,}\) this becomes \(40x^{2}+60x-20=0\text{.}\) This has solutions
    \begin{align*} x&=\frac{-3+\sqrt{17}}{4} & x&=\frac{-3-\sqrt{17}}{4}. \end{align*}
    Since we know \(0\le \theta \le \pi/2\text{,}\) we know that \(x=\cos(\theta)\ge 0\text{,}\) so \(\cos(\theta)=x=\frac{-3+\sqrt{17}}{4}\text{,}\) giving us \(\theta=\arccos\left(\frac{-3+\sqrt{17}}{4}\right)\text{.}\) This yields the volume
    \begin{align*} V\left(\arccos\left(\frac{-3+\sqrt{17}}{4}\right)\right) & =20 \left(\frac{-3+\sqrt{17}}{4}\right) \sqrt{1 -\left(\frac{-3+\sqrt{17}}{4}\right)^{2}} + 60 \sqrt{1 -\left(\frac{-3+\sqrt{17}}{4}\right)^{2}}\\ &\approx 62.9760258855007\text{ft}^{3}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Figure 56 shows the graphs \(y=f(x)\) (solid) and \(y=g(x)\) (dashed), one of which is the derivative of the other.
    🔗
    Determine which graph is the derivative and justify your answer in terms of the relationships between a function and its derivative.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The dashed function is the derivative of the solid function. One simple way to see this is that the maxima and minima of the solid function correspond to the zeroes of the dashed functions, whereas the reverse is not true.

    4. Computation and Patterns.

    In this problem, you will calculate several derivatives (using shortcuts) and look for patterns in the derivatives.
    🔗

    (a) Squares of Functions.

    (i)

    Calculate the derivative of \(x^{6}\) in two ways: first by using the pattern for derivatives of \(x^{r}\) directly, and then using the fact that \(x^{6}=\left(x^{3}\right)\left(x^{3}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    From the power rule, \(\left(x^{6}\right)^{'}=6x^{5}\text{.}\) Writing \(x^{6}=x^{3}x^{3}=f(x)g(x)\) where \(f(x)=g(x)=x^{3}\text{,}\) we can use the product rule,
    \begin{align*} \left(f(x)g(x)\right)^{'} &= f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x)\\ &= (3x^{2})(x^{3})+(x^{3})(3x^{2})\\ &= 3x^{5}+3x^{5}\\ &= 6x^{5}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (ii)

    Calculate the derivative of \(\nexp{2x}=\left(\nexp{x}\right)^{2}\) using the fact that \(\nexp{2x}=\nexp{x}\cdot{}\nexp{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(f(x)=g(x)=\nexp{x}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{align*} \left(f(x)g(x)\right)^{'} &= f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x)\\ &= (\nexp{x})(\nexp{x})+(\nexp{x})(\nexp{x})\\ &= 2\nexp{2x}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (iii)

    Calculate the derivative of \(\cos^{2}(x)\) using the fact that \(\cos^{2}(x)=\cos(x)\cdot{}\cos(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} \big(\cos(x)\cos(x)\big)^{'} &= (\cos(x))^{'}(\cos(x))+(\cos(x))(\cos(x))^{'}\\ &= (-\sin(x))(\cos(x))+(\cos(x))(-\sin(x))\\ &= -2\sin(x)\cos(x). \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (iv)

    Let \(f(x)\) be a differentiable function (with derivative \(f'(x)\)) and calculate the derivative of \(\left(f(x)\right)^{2}=f(x)\cdot{}f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Compare this result to your previous calculations.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Applying the pattern for products with \(f(x)=g(x)\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{align*} \big(f(x)f(x)\big)^{'} &= f(x)f'(x)+f'(x)f(x)\\ &=2f(x)f'(x). \end{align*}
    🔗
    If we let \(f(x)=\nexp{x}\text{,}\) we get \(\left(\nexp{2x}\right)^{'}=2\nexp{x}\nexp{x}=2\nexp{2x}\text{,}\) and if we let \(f(x)=\cos(x)\text{,}\) we get \(\left(\cos^{2}(x)\right)^{'}=2\cos(x)\cdot{}-\sin(x)=-2\sin(x)\cos(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Cubes of Functions.

    (i)

    Calculate the derivative of \(\nexp{3x}=\left(\nexp{x}\right)^{3}\) using your previous work and the fact that \(\nexp{3x}=\nexp{2x}\cdot{}\nexp{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(f(x)=\nexp{2x}\) and \(g(x)=\nexp{x}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} \frac{\di{}\nexp{3x}}{\di{}x} &= \left( \nexp{2x}\nexp{x}\right)^{'}\\ &= \left( f(x)g(x)\right)^{'}\\ &= f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x)\\ &= (2\nexp{2x})(\nexp{x})+\nexp{2x}\nexp{x}\\ &= 2\nexp{3x}+\nexp{3x}\\ &= 3\nexp{3x} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (ii)

    Calculate the derivative of \(\cos^{3}(x)\) using your previous work and the fact that \(\cos^{3}(x)=\cos^{2}(x)\cdot{}\cos(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(f(x)=\cos^{2}(x)\) and \(g(x)=\cos(x)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} \frac{\di{}\cos^{3}(x)}{\di{}x} &= \left( \cos^{2}(x)\cos(x)\right)^{'}\\ &= \left( f(x)g(x)\right)^{'}\\ &= f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x)\\ &= (-2\cos(x)\sin(x))(\cos(x))+\cos^{2}(x)(-\sin(x))\\ &= -2\cos^{2}(x)\sin(x)-\cos^{2}(x)\sin(x)\\ &= -3\cos^{2}(x)\sin(x) \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (iii)

    Let \(f(x)\) be a differentiable function (with derivative \(f'(x)\)) and calculate the derivative of \(\left(f(x)\right)^{3}=\left(f(x)\right)^{2}\cdot{}f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Compare this result to your previous calculations.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} \big(\left(f(x)\right)^{3}\big)^{'} &= \big(\left(f(x)\right)^{2}f(x)\big)^{'}\\ &= \big(\left(f(x)\right)^{2}\big)^{'}f(x)+\big(\left(f(x)\right)^{2}\big)f'(x)\\ &= \left(2f(x)f'(x)\right)f(x)+\big(\left(f(x)\right)^{2}\big)f'(x)\\ &= 2\left(f(x)\right)^{2}f'(x)+\left(f(x)\right)^{2}f'(x)\\ &= 3\left(f(x)\right)^{2}f'(x) \end{align*}
    🔗
    It’s easy to see that this is the same as for \(\left(\nexp{x}\right)^{3}\text{.}\) It takes a little more work to see that \(\left(\cos^{3}(x)\right)'=3\cos^{2}(x)(-\sin(x))=-3\cos^{2}(x)\sin(x)\text{,}\) which is the same as what we got earlier.
    🔗
